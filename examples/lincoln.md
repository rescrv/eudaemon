# Abraham Lincoln: A Life

Abraham Lincoln (February 12, 1809 – April 15, 1865) was an American lawyer, politician, and statesman who served as the 16th president of the United States from 1861 until his assassination in 1865. Lincoln led the nation through the American Civil War and succeeded in preserving the Union, abolishing slavery, bolstering the federal government, and modernizing the U.S. economy.

## Early Life

The story of Abraham Lincoln's rise from humble frontier origins to the highest office in the land remains one of the most compelling narratives in American history. His early years shaped the character that would later guide a nation through its darkest hours.

### Birth and Family Origins

Abraham Lincoln was born on February 12, 1809, in a one-room log cabin on Sinking Spring Farm near Hodgenville, Kentucky. He was the second child of Thomas Lincoln and Nancy Hanks Lincoln. His father was a respected community member who worked as a farmer and carpenter.

The Lincoln family traced their ancestry back to Samuel Lincoln, who emigrated from Hingham, Norfolk, England, to Hingham, Massachusetts, in 1637. The family subsequently moved through New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and Virginia before settling in Kentucky.

### Childhood in Kentucky

Lincoln's earliest memories were of the Kentucky frontier. The family lived in a succession of humble dwellings, moving from Sinking Spring Farm to Knob Creek when Abraham was two years old. At Knob Creek, young Abraham had his first experiences with formal education, attending ABC schools with his older sister Sarah.

Life on the frontier was harsh. Lincoln later recalled helping his father with farm work from an early age, planting seeds, carrying water, and performing other chores that built the physical strength he would be known for as an adult.

### Move to Indiana

In December 1816, when Abraham was seven years old, the Lincoln family crossed the Ohio River and settled in the wilderness of southern Indiana, near present-day Gentryville. The move was prompted partly by land title difficulties in Kentucky and partly by Thomas Lincoln's opposition to slavery.

The family's first winter in Indiana was spent in a crude half-faced camp—a three-sided shelter with an open fire in front. The following year, with help from Nancy's relatives, Thomas built a more permanent cabin.

### The Death of Nancy Hanks Lincoln

Tragedy struck the Lincoln family in the fall of 1818 when Nancy Hanks Lincoln died of milk sickness at age thirty-four. Abraham was only nine years old. The loss of his mother profoundly affected him, and he later described her as his "angel mother."

For over a year, the Lincoln children—Abraham and Sarah—were cared for primarily by their twelve-year-old cousin, Dennis Hanks, who had come to live with the family. The household struggled without a woman's presence.

### Sarah Bush Johnston Lincoln

In December 1819, Thomas Lincoln returned to Kentucky and married Sarah Bush Johnston, a widow with three children of her own. Sarah proved to be a loving stepmother who encouraged Abraham's education and intellectual development.

She brought books, furniture, and a sense of order to the Lincoln household. Abraham later credited her with nurturing his love of learning. She recognized his exceptional mind and encouraged him to read whenever possible.

## Education and Self-Improvement

Abraham Lincoln's formal education amounted to less than a year in aggregate, yet he became one of the most eloquent and well-read presidents in American history. His intellectual development was largely self-directed.

### Frontier Schooling

The schools Lincoln attended were "blab schools," where students recited their lessons aloud. He attended sporadically when teachers were available and when farm work permitted. His teachers included Zachariah Riney, Caleb Hazel, Azel Dorsey, and Andrew Crawford.

Despite the limited formal instruction, Lincoln learned to read, write, and perform basic arithmetic—skills that were by no means universal on the frontier. His handwriting, preserved in various documents, shows a clear, steady hand.

### Self-Education Through Reading

Lincoln's true education came through voracious reading. Books were scarce on the frontier, making each volume precious. He read and reread the family Bible, *Aesop's Fables*, *Robinson Crusoe*, *Pilgrim's Progress*, and a *History of the United States*.

One formative book was *Parson Weems's Life of Washington*, which Lincoln reportedly walked miles to borrow. The story of Washington's leadership during the Revolution deeply influenced his understanding of American ideals.

### Study of Law

Lincoln's interest in law began while he was still in Indiana. He attended trials at the local courthouse, fascinated by the proceedings. After moving to Illinois, he began serious study of the law.

He borrowed law books from John Todd Stuart and other Springfield attorneys. He read *Blackstone's Commentaries on the Laws of England*, *Chitty's Pleadings*, and other legal texts by candlelight after long days of manual labor.

In 1836, Lincoln was admitted to the Illinois bar. He had taught himself law entirely through self-study, a remarkable achievement that demonstrated his determination and intellectual capacity.

## Early Career

Lincoln's professional life before entering national politics encompassed a variety of occupations that exposed him to different aspects of American society and economy.

### Manual Labor and Commerce

As a young man, Lincoln worked as a farmhand, rail-splitter, and flatboatman. At nineteen, he made his first trip to New Orleans on a flatboat, traveling down the Ohio and Mississippi rivers. This journey exposed him to the broader world beyond the frontier.

In 1831, Lincoln made a second flatboat voyage to New Orleans. It was during this trip, according to family tradition, that he witnessed a slave auction that made a deep impression on him.

### New Salem Years

In 1831, Lincoln settled in New Salem, Illinois, a small frontier village on the Sangamon River. He worked as a store clerk for Denton Offutt, where his honesty and good humor earned him the nickname "Honest Abe."

When Offutt's store failed, Lincoln tried various occupations: store owner (the business failed, leaving him in debt), postmaster, and surveyor. He also served briefly in the Illinois militia during the Black Hawk War of 1832, though he saw no combat.

### Entry into Politics

Lincoln's first attempt at elected office came in 1832, when he ran for the Illinois General Assembly as a Whig candidate. Though he lost the election, he received nearly every vote in his home precinct of New Salem—evidence of his local popularity.

Two years later, in 1834, Lincoln won election to the Illinois House of Representatives. He would serve four successive terms, becoming a skilled parliamentarian and leader of the Whig minority.

## Law Practice

Lincoln's legal career spanned over two decades and established his reputation as one of Illinois' most capable attorneys. His practice took him across the Eighth Judicial Circuit, where he developed the political relationships that would later support his rise to national prominence.

### Partnership with John Todd Stuart

Lincoln's first law partnership was with John Todd Stuart, who had encouraged his legal studies. The firm of Stuart and Lincoln operated from 1837 to 1841, handling a variety of civil and criminal cases.

Stuart's political ambitions—he was elected to Congress in 1838—meant that Lincoln often handled the firm's business alone. This experience quickly developed his courtroom skills and legal knowledge.

### Partnership with Stephen T. Logan

From 1841 to 1844, Lincoln practiced with Stephen T. Logan, one of the most accomplished lawyers in Illinois. Logan was a demanding mentor who pushed Lincoln to improve his preparation and legal reasoning.

The partnership with Logan marked Lincoln's development into a more polished and effective attorney. He learned the importance of thorough preparation and precise legal argumentation.

### Lincoln and Herndon

In 1844, Lincoln formed a partnership with William H. Herndon that would last until Lincoln's death. Herndon was nine years younger than Lincoln and had read law in the Logan and Lincoln office.

The partnership was unusual in that Lincoln was clearly the senior partner, yet he insisted on an equal division of fees. Herndon managed the office while Lincoln traveled the circuit.

### Circuit Riding

Twice a year, Lincoln traveled the Eighth Judicial Circuit with Judge David Davis and other lawyers. The circuit covered a vast territory in central Illinois, requiring weeks of travel through frontier conditions.

These trips were essential to Lincoln's political development. He shared meals, lodging, and conversation with lawyers, judges, and citizens across the region. The relationships he formed would prove invaluable to his political career.

## Political Rise

Lincoln's political career evolved from state legislator to congressman to presidential candidate. His views on slavery and union became increasingly defined through this period of national crisis.

### Illinois Legislature

During his four terms in the Illinois House of Representatives (1834–1842), Lincoln established himself as a capable legislator. He worked to move the state capital from Vandalia to Springfield and supported internal improvements.

In 1837, Lincoln was one of only two legislators to sign a protest against slavery—a mild document by later standards, but notable in its time. His opposition to slavery was consistent throughout his career, though his approach to addressing it evolved.

### Marriage to Mary Todd

In 1842, Lincoln married Mary Todd, a member of a prominent Kentucky family. Mary was educated, ambitious, and politically engaged. Their courtship had been tumultuous, including a broken engagement, but they eventually married on November 4, 1842.

The Lincolns would have four sons: Robert Todd (1843), Edward Baker (1846), William Wallace (1850), and Thomas "Tad" (1853). Only Robert would survive to adulthood.

### Congressional Term

Lincoln served a single term in the United States House of Representatives (1847–1849). He was an active congressman, introducing the "Spot Resolutions" challenging President Polk's justification for the Mexican-American War.

His opposition to the war was unpopular in Illinois, and he honored his promise not to seek re-election. He returned to Springfield and his law practice, seemingly done with politics.

### Return to Politics

The Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854, which allowed slavery to expand into territories where it had been prohibited, drew Lincoln back into politics. He delivered powerful speeches against the act and its author, Senator Stephen A. Douglas.

Lincoln joined the new Republican Party in 1856. The party united former Whigs, Free Soilers, and anti-slavery Democrats in opposition to the expansion of slavery.

### Lincoln-Douglas Debates

In 1858, Lincoln challenged Douglas for his Senate seat. The campaign featured seven debates across Illinois that attracted national attention. Lincoln articulated his opposition to slavery's expansion while carefully distinguishing his position from abolitionism.

Though Lincoln lost the election (senators were chosen by state legislatures at the time), the debates elevated his national profile. His speeches were published as a book that circulated widely in the North.

## Presidential Campaign of 1860

Lincoln's nomination and election in 1860 transformed American politics and set the stage for civil war.

### The Cooper Union Speech

On February 27, 1860, Lincoln delivered a speech at Cooper Union in New York City that established him as a serious presidential contender. He methodically examined the Founders' views on federal power over slavery in the territories.

The speech concluded with the memorable exhortation: "Let us have faith that right makes might, and in that faith, let us, to the end, dare to do our duty as we understand it."

### Republican Nomination

The Republican National Convention met in Chicago in May 1860. Lincoln was not the frontrunner—that position belonged to Senator William H. Seward of New York. But Lincoln's moderate position on slavery and his lack of political enemies made him an attractive compromise candidate.

On the third ballot, Lincoln secured the nomination. His campaign adopted the image of "The Rail Splitter," emphasizing his humble origins and self-made success.

### Election Victory

Lincoln won the presidency on November 6, 1860, with about 40 percent of the popular vote in a four-way race. He carried every free state except New Jersey, which he split with Douglas.

His election was the signal for Southern secession. South Carolina voted to leave the Union on December 20, 1860, followed by six other Deep South states before Lincoln's inauguration.

## The Civil War

The four years of Lincoln's presidency were dominated by the most destructive war in American history. His leadership during this crisis defined his historical legacy.

### Secession Crisis

Between Lincoln's election and his inauguration, seven Southern states seceded and formed the Confederate States of America. Lincoln rejected the legality of secession, viewing the Southern states as still part of the Union.

President James Buchanan remained in office until March 4, 1861, and took no action to prevent secession. Lincoln waited powerlessly in Springfield, assembling his cabinet and preparing to assume power.

### Fort Sumter

The crisis came to a head at Fort Sumter in Charleston Harbor. The federal garrison was running low on supplies, and Lincoln had to decide whether to reinforce, resupply, or evacuate the fort.

Lincoln chose to send supplies but not reinforcements. Confederate forces fired on the supply ships and then bombarded the fort on April 12, 1861. The Civil War had begun.

### Mobilization

Lincoln called for 75,000 volunteers to suppress the rebellion. This call prompted four more states—Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee, and North Carolina—to join the Confederacy.

The Union faced enormous challenges: a divided nation, an inadequate army, and a population uncertain about the war's aims. Lincoln had to build a military force, maintain domestic support, and prevent foreign intervention.

### Military Strategy

Lincoln involved himself deeply in military strategy. He read books on military tactics and frequently visited army camps and headquarters. His strategic insight improved throughout the war.

He understood before many of his generals that the war could only be won by destroying Confederate armies, not by capturing territory. He also grasped the importance of simultaneous pressure on multiple fronts.

### Emancipation Proclamation

Lincoln issued the preliminary Emancipation Proclamation on September 22, 1862, following the Union victory at Antietam. The final proclamation, issued January 1, 1863, declared slaves in rebel states "forever free."

The proclamation transformed the war from a struggle to preserve the Union into a fight for human freedom. It also authorized the enlistment of African American soldiers, who would eventually number nearly 200,000.

### Gettysburg Address

On November 19, 1863, Lincoln delivered brief remarks at the dedication of the Soldiers' National Cemetery in Gettysburg, Pennsylvania. The 272-word speech redefined the meaning of the war and the nation's founding principles.

"Four score and seven years ago our fathers brought forth on this continent, a new nation, conceived in Liberty, and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal."

### Re-election of 1864

Lincoln's re-election was far from certain. War weariness, military setbacks, and political opposition threatened his campaign. The Democratic nominee, General George McClellan, ran on a peace platform.

Military victories in the fall of 1864—particularly Sherman's capture of Atlanta—revived Union morale. Lincoln won decisively, carrying every state except Kentucky, Delaware, and New Jersey.

### End of the War

By early 1865, the Confederacy was collapsing. Grant's army besieged Petersburg and Richmond, while Sherman marched through the Carolinas. On April 9, Robert E. Lee surrendered to Grant at Appomattox Court House.

Lincoln visited Richmond on April 4, walking through the former Confederate capital. Freed slaves gathered around him, some kneeling, which Lincoln discouraged: "Don't kneel to me. That is not right. You must kneel to God only."

## Assassination

Lincoln's murder on April 14, 1865, transformed him from a controversial wartime president into a martyred symbol of national unity and emancipation.

### Ford's Theatre

On the evening of April 14, Good Friday, Lincoln attended a performance of *Our American Cousin* at Ford's Theatre in Washington. His wife Mary accompanied him, along with guests Major Henry Rathbone and Clara Harris.

At approximately 10:15 p.m., John Wilkes Booth entered the presidential box and shot Lincoln in the back of the head with a .44 caliber derringer pistol.

### Death

Lincoln never regained consciousness. He was carried across the street to the Petersen House, where he was placed in a bed too short for his frame. Doctors attended him through the night.

Abraham Lincoln died at 7:22 a.m. on April 15, 1865. Secretary of War Edwin Stanton reportedly said, "Now he belongs to the ages."

### National Mourning

Lincoln's death prompted an outpouring of grief unprecedented in American history. His body lay in state at the Capitol and was then carried by train to Springfield, Illinois, for burial.

The funeral train traveled through 180 cities and seven states, allowing millions to pay their respects. Lincoln was buried at Oak Ridge Cemetery in Springfield on May 4, 1865.

## Legacy

Abraham Lincoln is consistently ranked among the greatest American presidents. His leadership during the Civil War preserved the Union and abolished slavery, reshaping the nation's trajectory.

### Impact on American Government

Lincoln's presidency expanded federal power and established important precedents. His suspension of habeas corpus, military draft, and economic policies significantly strengthened the national government.

The Thirteenth Amendment, which Lincoln championed, permanently abolished slavery. He also signed legislation establishing land-grant colleges, the transcontinental railroad, and a national banking system.

### Memorial and Commemoration

The Lincoln Memorial in Washington, D.C., dedicated in 1922, has become one of America's most recognizable landmarks. Lincoln's image appears on the penny and the five-dollar bill.

His birthday was long celebrated as a holiday, now incorporated into Presidents' Day. Mount Rushmore features his likeness alongside Washington, Jefferson, and Theodore Roosevelt.

### Historical Assessment

Historians consistently rank Lincoln as America's greatest president. His moral vision, political skill, and eloquence continue to inspire study and admiration.

His words remain touchstones of American democracy: "government of the people, by the people, for the people" and "with malice toward none, with charity for all."
